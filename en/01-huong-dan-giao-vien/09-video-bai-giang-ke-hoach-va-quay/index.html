Lesson videos (1): Planning and filming

Draft

Types of lesson video, the one video – one objective principle, a script template, shot lists, camera angles for guitar, audio, lighting and how to present on camera.

Objectives

  • Teachers know which videos to film for each lesson and which learning objective each video serves.
  • Teachers can write a script outline and a shot list before switching the camera on.
  • The footage clearly shows both hands and clearly captures the guitar sound, good enough to edit (see Lesson videos (2)).

Why an online course needs lesson videos

  • Live Zoom lessons compress the picture and sound and add latency — a pre-recorded video gives students a sharp, true-sounding model.
  • Flipped classroom: students watch the demo video before the lesson → lesson time goes on corrections and guided practice, not 15 minutes of demonstrating.
  • Students can rewatch as often as they like, slow it down and loop the hard part — things you cannot do in a live lesson.
  • A good video works for many classes over many years → a one-off investment.

  • Every lesson in the curriculum already contains media blocks — together they are the list of videos to film. The command cargo run -- report --csv > media.csv exports the whole list to Excel / Google Sheets for assigning the work.

Video design principles

  • One video – one objective. If the title needs an "and" ("The C chord and strumming pattern B"), split it into 2 videos.
  • Keep it short: a study of millions of course-video views (edX, Guo – Kim – Rubin, 2014) found viewers rarely watch beyond ~6 minutes; skill videos should be 1–3 minutes, full-lesson videos no more than 10 minutes. Longer → split into parts.
  • Let them hear the goal first: the first 10–20 seconds are a demo at real tempo — students know what they are learning it for.
  • Students must do something: every 2–3 minutes, include a call to action ("Pause the video, fret C, pluck each string").
  • Terminology matches the lesson page: same technique names, same finger numbers, same symbols, same chord names. The first time a term is used: Vietnamese + English/Italian in brackets, just as in the written curriculum.
  • Videos don't replace the lesson page: the video shows, the lesson page records (tab, diagrams, criteria). The video always mentions "The tab and exercises are on the Lesson 8 page".

Step 1 — Script (an outline, not word-for-word)

Reading out a fully written script sounds stiff. Write a bullet-point outline + the key sentences that must be said exactly.

Full-lesson video structure template (6–8 minutes)

0:20–0:45Objectives"After this video, you'll be able to…" (1–2 objectives, taken from the lesson page)Wide shot, looking into the lens
0:45–1:00Prior-knowledge check"You need to be able to play a clean Am before this lesson"Wide shot
1:00–4:00Break it down – slow demoStep by step: place the fingers → check each string → play slowly; every step ends with "now you try"Left-hand close-up / over the shoulder + chord diagram overlay
4:00–5:00Common mistakes2–3 mistakes, each one: wrong → right, side by sideClose-up

6:30–7:00Summary + homework3 key points, homework, "send me your video before …day"Wide shot, looking into the lens

Phrases that work

  • Opening: "This is what you'll be able to play after this lesson." → play straight away, no long greeting.
  • Left-hand directions: always give finger – string – fret: "Finger 3, string 5, fret 3."
  • Positions: use string numbers, not "top string / bottom string" (easy to confuse: string 1 is at the bottom when you hold the guitar but at the top in tab).
  • Call to action: "Pause the video here, try it 5 times, then carry on watching."
  • Closing: "Remember: slow and clean beats fast and buzzy."
  • For Vietnamese-language videos, use the Vietnamese wording from the Vietnamese version of this page (e.g. "Ngón 3, dây 5, phím 3").

  • Don't flip (mirror) the image in the edit: the writing on the guitar comes out backwards, and both left- and right-handed students get more confused. Instead, use the over-the-shoulder angle for finger placement, and always refer to finger numbers and string numbers.
  • Framing: the guitar sits diagonally in the frame; leave 1/3 of the frame empty (usually on the right) for chord diagrams / tab in the edit.
  • Film landscape 16:9 for lesson videos. Portrait 9:16 is only for short promo clips (Zalo, TikTok, Facebook Reels).
  • One camera or several? Start with 2 cameras (wide + over the shoulder / left-hand close-up); add a 3rd for the right hand once you are used to the workflow.

Step 4 — Camera / phone settings

  • Resolution: 1920×1080 (1080p). Use 4K only if you want to crop in during editing and your computer is powerful enough.
  • Frame rate: 30 fps for talking and demos; 60 fps for fast techniques (tremolo, rasgueado, strumming) so they stay smooth when slowed to 50%.
  • Anti-flicker: Vietnam's mains is 50 Hz → shutter speed 1/50 or 1/100 at 30 fps, 1/100 at 60 fps (video apps such as Blackmagic Camera or Filmic, or the phone's "Pro" mode, let you set this).
  • Lock focus and exposure (press and hold on the phone screen → AE/AF Lock) — otherwise the focus will keep jumping to follow your hands.
  • Fixed white balance, the same value on every camera (e.g. 5600K) → skin tones match between angles.
  • Phone: turn on airplane mode / Do Not Disturb, have enough battery or keep it plugged in, and at least 20 GB free (1 minute of 1080p60 ≈ 150–200 MB).
  • Turn on the grid to keep the guitar neck lined up between takes.

Step 5 — Audio: more important than the picture

Viewers will put up with a slightly soft picture, but abandon the video straight away if the guitar sounds buzzy, quiet or echoey.

  • Record the guitar separately with a good mic (see Equipment and teaching room): 30–50 cm from the guitar, aimed at frets 12–14.
  • Record the voice separately with a clip-on (lavalier) mic, wired or wireless → you can balance voice and guitar in the edit.
  • Record into the audio interface / computer (Audacity, Reaper) alongside the cameras; the cameras' own audio is only used for syncing.
  • Recording level: peaks around -12 to -6 dBFS when playing at your loudest; never hit 0 dB (clipping).
  • Switch off fans, air conditioners and any fridge near the room; close the windows (motorbikes, horns); film at quiet times (early morning, late evening).
  • Clap once, clearly, before every shot: it creates a spike in the audio waveform and a visual cue for syncing multiple cameras in the edit.
  • Record 10 seconds of silent "room tone" at the start of the session — used for noise reduction in the edit.
  • Guitar: change the strings 2–3 days beforehand (brand-new strings are too bright and go out of tune easily); retune before every shot.

Step 6 — Lighting and set

  • Key light at 45° in front of you; a weaker fill light on the other side to soften shadows; a small lamp aimed just at the neck.
  • Don't mix window light (bluish white) with yellow bulbs → patchy skin tones.
  • A glossy guitar top reflects the lights → angle the guitar slightly or raise the lights.
  • Plain clothes in a colour that contrasts with the guitar (light wood guitar → dark top); avoid fine stripes (they shimmer on camera); roll up your sleeves so your wrists are visible.
  • Take off rings, watches and bracelets — they clatter and hide the wrist.
  • A tidy background, the same across videos (Agora Academy brand identity).

Step 7 — Presenting on camera

  • Look into the lens when talking to students; look at the guitar when demonstrating.
  • Speak 10–20% slower than normal, in short sentences; with a little more energy than in a live lesson.
  • Count in loud and clear before every demo passage: "One – two – three – four".
  • Demonstrate in this order: real tempo → slow → very slow, step by step → real tempo again.
  • Leave 2 seconds of silence before and after each spoken or played section — the editor needs room to cut.
  • Mistake halfway through: stop, stay silent for 2 seconds, say "again" and restart from the beginning of the sentence — don't fix it mid-sentence.
  • Read a "slate" before every take: "Lesson 8, scene 6, take 2" ("Bài 8, cảnh 6, lần 2" when filming in Vietnamese) — it makes finding files much faster.

Filming-day checklist

  • Script outline + shot list printed / open on a tablet.
  • Strings changed 2–3 days ago, guitar tuned, right-hand nails filed (Classical track).
  • Cameras: 1080p, right fps for each type of shot, focus / exposure / white balance locked, airplane mode, enough battery + storage.
  • Audio: guitar mic + voice mic, levels -12 to -6 dBFS, monitoring on headphones, fans / air conditioning off.
  • Lighting: no backlighting, no flicker, the neck well lit.
  • Rings and watch off; plain clothes; tidy background.
  • Metronome on headphones (so the click doesn't bleed into the mic) — or use the app's flashing light.
  • Do a 20-second test recording and play it back on headphones to check there is sound before the real take.

Common mistakes

  • Filming one long 30-minute take "to get it over with" → very slow to edit, hard to fix one part.
  • Only a wide shot → students can't see the left-hand fingers.
  • Forgetting to lock focus → the picture keeps going in and out of focus following the hands.
  • The metronome bleeding into the mic → impossible to remove in the edit.
  • Talking a lot, playing little — an 8-minute video with only 1 minute of guitar.
  • A long introduction of yourself, the channel and the course at the start of every video.
  • Using different terms from the lesson page → students get confused.
