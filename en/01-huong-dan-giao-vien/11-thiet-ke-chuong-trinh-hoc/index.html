Curriculum design

Draft

Principles and process for designing a guitar course: backward design from outcomes, writing measurable objectives, ordering lessons by prerequisite skills, cognitive load, spiral review, rubric-based assessment, differentiation and data-driven improvement.

Objectives

  • Teachers understand why the Agora curriculum is ordered the way it is, so they can teach flexibly without breaking the structure.
  • Teachers can design a new lesson, a short course (e.g. "Crash course in song accompaniment, 8 sessions") or revise an existing lesson to the same standard.
  • There is a shared process for reviewing and improving the curriculum every term.

1. Backward design

Design from the end result back to the first lesson, not from "what does lesson 1 teach" (the Understanding by Design model — Wiggins & McTighe).

  1. Desired results: after the course, what can students do? (not what do they "know")
  2. Evidence: how do we know students can do it? → tests, rubrics, videos.
  3. Learning activities: which lessons, exercises and videos lead students to that evidence?

Level 1 example:

  • Result: "Accompany a whole song with 3–4 chords and a basic strumming pattern, without stopping" (Level 1).
  • Evidence: items 5–8 of the Lesson 12 rubric.
  • Activities: Lessons 7, 8 (chords) → Lesson 9 (rhythm) → Lesson 10 (strumming patterns, putting a song together).

Check rule: every item in the end-of-level rubric must be taught and practised in at least 2 earlier lessons; every lesson must serve at least 1 rubric item. A lesson that serves none → consider turning it into a supplementary lesson.

2. Writing measurable objectives

A good objective = observable verb + condition + criterion.

WeakGood
Understand the C chordFret C, 5 strings ringing clearly, without strumming string 6
Know the Ballad rhythmAccompany the C – Am – F – G progression in the Ballad rhythm, tempo 70, 4 rounds without stopping
Get familiar with the metronomePlay 1-2-3-4 in time with the metronome at 60, one note per beat, 3 times through
Develop a better feel for musicTell apart major / minor chords, 8/10 correct
  • Verbs to use: play, fret, accompany, arpeggiate, change, read, count, recognise, tell apart, keep (time), tune unaided, perform.
  • Verbs to avoid: understand, know, get familiar with, feel, grasp — they can't be observed.
  • No more than 3 objectives per lesson. More → the lesson is overloaded, or should be split.
  • Objectives → turn them straight into the "Completion criteria" (checkboxes) at the end of the lesson page.

3. Prerequisite skill map

Before ordering the lessons, list which skills must come before which.

Posture (L2) ─┬─> Right hand (L4) ────────────────────────────────────────────────┬─> Strumming (L10) ─> Full song
              └─> Left hand (L5) ─> Diagrams (L6) ─> Em, Am (L7) ─> C, G, D (L8) ─┘
Tuning (L3) ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────> every lesson
Rhythm, metronome (L9) ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────> L10, L11, L12
  • L = Lesson (L2 = Lesson 2); "Diagrams" = reading tab and chord diagrams.
  • Skills that sit on many paths (posture, tuning, rhythm) must be taught early and reviewed in every lesson.
  • When adding a new lesson: work out which lessons it requires and which it unlocks — number it accordingly.
  • Students transferring from elsewhere / self-taught: run the placement check against the nodes on the map (Placement assessment).

4. Ordering content: principles

  • One new difficulty at a time: if the new material is in the left hand, the right hand keeps a familiar pattern, and vice versa. (Lesson 7: new chords + simple downstrokes; Lesson 10: familiar chords + a new strumming pattern.)
  • Simple → complex, slow → fast, separate → combined: left hand alone → right hand alone → both hands → add the metronome → add singing.
  • Early win: students must be able to play "something that sounds like music" within the first 2–3 sessions (Em – Am with downstrokes), and a whole song by around session 10. Adults drop out most in weeks 3–6 if they still can't play anything that "sounds like a song".
  • Spiral curriculum: each skill comes back at the next level with greater difficulty:
    • Open-string arpeggios (Level 1) → p-i-m-a arpeggios (Level 2) → fingerstyle accompaniment (Level 3) → tremolo (Level 4).
    • ↓↑ strumming (Level 1) → Ballad, Bolero (Level 2) → Bossa Nova, Rumba (Level 3) → Rasgueado (Level 4).
  • Spaced review: every session has 10 minutes of review — not just the previous lesson, but also lessons from 2–4 weeks ago.
  • Interleaving: after learning each chord on its own, practise them mixed up (random chord progressions) — harder in practice but remembered longer.
  • Theory follows practice: play first, explain afterwards ("why Am → C moves only 1 finger" — Supplement 3).

5. Cognitive load in a session

  • Learners' working memory can only hold a few new pieces of information at once — a beginner guitarist is thinking about fingers, rhythm and sound all at the same time.
  • Each session: 1–2 new skills, the rest is review and practice.
  • Break skills into small steps that students can succeed at straight away (place finger 3 → add finger 2 → add finger 1 → pluck each string).
  • The "I do – we do – you do" sequence, online version:
    • I do: demonstrate at real tempo + slowly (or a lesson video watched beforehand).
    • We do: call and response in small chunks (no playing at the same time over the internet).
    • You do: the student plays with a metronome on their own device, the teacher mutes and observes.
  • If the student can answer "What did you learn today?" in 1 sentence → the session was focused enough.

6. Designing practice and homework

  • Homework = deliberate practice (Supplement 2): small, measurable goals, with feedback.
  • Every exercise has a number: tempo, repetitions, rounds, minutes ("1 minute of Am – C chord changes, count how many").
  • Total homework time matches how long students really practise (Level 1: 20–30 minutes/day; children 10–15).
  • Always include 1 "just for fun" part — a song the student likes — to keep motivation up.
  • Homework submitted as a short video → the teacher has evidence for assessing progress (Homework and feedback).

7. Assessment: ongoing and end of level

  • Formative assessment: every session — the "Completion criteria" checkboxes, homework videos, observation on camera. Purpose: adjusting the teaching, not giving marks.
  • Summative assessment: session 12 — rubric, decision on moving up a level.
  • Designing a good rubric:
    • 3–4 levels (0–1–2 or 1–2–3–4), each level describing something observable, not "fair / good".
    • Each rubric item matches 1 objective that was taught; don't test what hasn't been taught.
    • A clear pass threshold + a rule for items scoring 0 (see the Lesson 12 rubric).
    • Two teachers marking the same video → a difference of ≤ 1 point per item. A bigger difference → rewrite the rubric descriptions.
  • Online: assess the sound from pre-recorded videos, assess posture via the live camera.

8. Differentiation: one curriculum, many kinds of student

  • Tracks: chung (Core) → everyone; co-dien (Classical) / dem-hat (Accompaniment) → by goal. Level 1 is shared, the split becomes clear from Level 2.
  • Fast students: give extra exercises from the Level 1 practice material bank (★★★), don't skip lessons — foundation skills still need enough time.
  • Slower students: split 1 lesson over 2 sessions (allowed in Level 1), lower the target tempo, keep the lesson order.
  • Children: 10-minute blocks, games, parental support; lower target tempos.
  • Working adults: short, realistic homework; songs they like from early on.
  • Write differentiation into the lesson's "Online teaching notes" section; don't create separate lessons for each group.

9. Choosing songs and pieces

Criteria, in order of priority:

  1. Serves the lesson's technical objective (uses only chords / techniques already learned + at most 1 new thing).
  2. Students want to play it — Vietnamese music (V-pop, ballads, bolero, Trịnh Công Sơn songs…), well-known international songs.
  3. Copyright: shared materials give only song title + chords; tab / sheet music only for public domain works (Sor, Carulli, Giuliani, Tárrega, Aguado, folk songs…). Never copy song lyrics.
  4. Variety: 4/4 and 3/4 time, major and minor, slow and medium tempo.
  • The song list is updated every term in Appendix 02.

11. Process for writing a new curriculum lesson

  1. Identify its place on the skill map (what it needs first, what it unlocks next) and the rubric item(s) it serves.
  2. Create the file from the template: cargo run -- new vi/02-cap-1-nhap-mon/18-ten-bai "Bài 18: Tiêu đề" (Vietnamese is the primary language; the example creates "Lesson 18: Title").
  3. Write it following the standard template (section 3 of docs/CONTENT_GUIDE.md in the source repository): Objectives → Preparation → Main content → Lesson flow → Online teaching notes → Common mistakes → Homework → Completion criteria → Media.
  4. Use notation blocks (chord, tab, strum, fretboard) instead of photos — easy to edit, consistent with the videos.
  5. Add a media block for every video / image / audio recording that is needed — even if it hasn't been filmed yet.
  6. Trial the lesson with 1–2 students and note how long each part really takes.
  7. Change to status = "review"; another teacher reads it and gives feedback using the checklist below.
  8. Revise, change to status = "ready"; translate into English (content/en/, same path).

Lesson review checklist (before changing to ready)

  • ≤ 3 objectives, each with an observable verb and a criterion.
  • Each objective has 1 matching line in "Completion criteria".
  • Only 1–2 new skills; prerequisite skills were taught in earlier lessons.
  • The lesson flow adds up to the right length (duration), and the student plays ≥ 50% of the time.
  • There is a review part; there is an "Online teaching notes" section (call and response, camera angles).
  • Homework has numbers (tempo, repetitions, time) and fits realistic practice time.
  • Terminology consistent with Appendix 01; finger numbers and diagrams correct.
  • No song lyrics, no copyrighted tab / sheet music.
  • track set to the right track; media blocks for the goal video and skill videos.

12. Data-driven curriculum improvement

At the end of each term, the teaching team meets once to look at the data:

DataWhere it comes fromSign that something needs fixing
Scores per rubric item at end of levelAssessment forms (Appendix 04)> 30% of students ≤ 1 point on the same item → the lesson teaching that skill needs revising / an extra session
Lessons split over 2 sessionsTeachers' progress logsThe same lesson split for > 1/3 of students → the lesson is overloaded, divide it differently
Homework video submission rateClass Drive / ZaloDrops sharply after a particular lesson → the homework is too hard / too long
Students dropping outClass listsConcentrated in one week → revisit the "early win" and the difficulty of that stretch
Video audience retentionYouTube AnalyticsDrops sharply at one point → the video is unclear, remake that section
Recurring questionsZalo, lessons≥ 3 students ask the same question → add it to the lesson or the FAQ for beginners
  • Record every change in the git history (a commit) with the reason: "Lesson 8: moved G–D into a separate practice section because 40% of students stopped > 2 beats".
  • Change a little, measure again: each term fix the 2–3 biggest issues; don't rewrite everything.

Common curriculum design mistakes

  • Cramming too much into one lesson because "students are paying, so we have to teach a lot".
  • Teaching music theory for several sessions straight before letting students play.
  • Testing things at the end of the level that were never practised (e.g. fast note reading when only tab was taught).
  • Only technical exercises, no songs → motivation drops; or only songs, no technique → students stall at Level 2.
  • Every teacher teaching their own way and reordering lessons at will → students who change class end up with gaps.
  • Never revising the curriculum even when the data shows a lesson is always "hard".
